Each area is one sixth of your score, so none can be skipped. Q1**Groups**16 of 16 Q2**Linear algebra**16 of 16 Q3**Rings**usually Q4**Modules**usually Q5**Fields & Galois**16 of 16 Q6**Representations**16 of 16 ### What question 2 actually asks Number of exams (out of 16, plus 2 MA papers) where each topic is the heart of the linear algebra question. Some questions count twice. ### What this means for you - **The minimal polynomial is the master key.** Diagonalizable ⇔ minimal polynomial has distinct roots solves a third of these questions. - **Know Jordan form by ranks.** Be able to list every Jordan form for given constraints and decide similarity from ranks of (A − λI)k. - **Invariant subspaces recur.** Practice relating T, T|W and T on V/W through their minimal and characteristic polynomials and traces. - **Mostly proofs, few computations.** Expect "show that" questions over ℂ, at the level of Hoffman–Kunze ch. 6–8. - **It hides in other questions too.** Galois automorphisms as linear maps (Jan 2007 #5), Schur's lemma via an eigenspace (Aug 2007 #6), ρ(g) diagonalizable because gn=1 (Jan 2003 #6). - **Rarely tested here:** bilinear forms, exterior algebra and determinants beyond basics. Learn them, but don't drill them. ### Most-repeated patterns in the other five questions #### Q1 · Groups Sylow counting to rule out simple groups (orders 90, 168, 153); groups of order pq, p²q; p-groups; permutation representations and Cayley's theorem. #### Q3 · Rings Artinian rings (three times); nilradical and Jacobson radical; prime avoidance; Zorn's lemma on ideals; localization. #### Q4 · Modules Injective and projective modules in diagram problems (five times); exact sequences and finite generation; tensor products and flatness; free modules over PIDs. #### Q5 · Fields Kummer extensions from xn − a (three times); cyclotomic subfields; finite fields; Galois groups of order pq or degree 15; prime-degree polynomials. #### Q6 · Representations Completing a character table (the A5 table twice); "the permutation representation contains the trivial one exactly once" (four times); proving pieces of Schur's lemma; decomposing a given representation. Your definitions & theorems book ## Make the book a recall tool The book is a good idea. Its value comes from writing it from memory and quizzing yourself with it, not from rereading it. Format each entry so the statement can be covered. Sample entry · G2 Sylow's Theorems Cover line Fold here. Name and section on top, everything below hidden when you quiz. Statement |G| = pam, p∤m. Sylow p-subgroups exist, are all conjugate, and np ≡ 1 mod p with np | m. Proof idea One or two lines: act on pa-subsets / act by conjugation on Sylp. Why each hypothesis What fails without it. A counterexample if there is one. Go-to example S4: n2=3, n3=4. Qual pattern "No simple group of order N": count elements across Sylow subgroups. Links Class equation (G1), semidirect products (G2), solvability of groups of order pq. ### Rules for writing it - **Generate, then check.** Read, close the source, write the entry, then correct in a second color. The corrections show you what to drill. - **Keep it lean.** Statements, hypotheses, one example, one counterexample, a proof idea. Full proofs belong in your problem notebook. - **Make cards as you go.** One flashcard per entry (paper or Anki). The book is the reference; the cards are how you use it. - **Mark it up from mistakes.** When a problem trips you, add a star and a one-line note to the relevant entry. - **Finish it by Dec 13.** After that it stays frozen except for error-log notes, so December goes to exams. Is this backed by research? ## The research ### KeepBuilding the book Writing your own summaries from memory uses the **generation effect**: information you produce is remembered better than information you only read. Explaining why each hypothesis matters is **self-explanation**, which reliably improves understanding in math and science. The book also gives you a clean source for flashcards. ### AdjustSaving recall and problems for December This is the part to change. Spacing practice out beats cramming it, and testing yourself beats rereading; both are among the strongest findings in learning science. Waiting until December puts all retrieval into one massed block and leaves October's material unpracticed for two months. The exam also tests problem solving, and you get better at what you practice.
